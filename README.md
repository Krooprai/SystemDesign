##Reflection

Generally, I was working for a client called DriverPass. Their whole thing is helping people who keep failing their driving test actually pass it. They wanted me to build them a system where customers could sign up, take some online classes, do practice tests, and book real in-person driving lessons with a real instructor. It’s not just a website but it is really the backend for their whole business-like booking lessons, matching people with cars and drivers, tracking packages, etc. The main point of contact was Liam (the owner) and Ian (IT guy). I think I did a solid job translating what the client was saying into real requirements. Therefore, they did not come to me saying “Liam wants to know who canceled a reservation” and I had to turn that into real functional requirements and a real system design. So, I feel like I was good at catching the small details they mentioned as an afterthought and making sure those actually showed up in the design instead of getting lost.
	I would most likely go back and clean up my diagrams a bit more like my activity diagrams and class diagram could  have been more consistent with each other. Ehen you are moving fast among the use case diagram, activity diagrams, and class diagram it is easy for things to drift a little, so next time I would slow down and double check that each requirement really traces all the diagrams. Also, I basically kept going back to “who is really going to use this and what to do they need to do.” Customers needed something easy like book a lesson, pick a package, see their progress, etc., but Liam and Ian needed totally different things like admin access, activity logs, and the ability to turn packages on or off without any hesitation or error by the system. So, I had to design the system with separate roles and permissions baked in from the start, not bolted on later. This part matters so much because if you don’t really think on the different users, you might end up building something that looks fine on paper but does not really fit how people will use it daily.
	My approach is generally talk to the client like a normal person first, don’t jump into diagrams right away. I like to try to pull out the real requirements from what they are saying before I even open a design tool. Then, I go functional versus nonfunctional, then use case diagrams to see the big image, after that activity diagrams to get into the weeds of each flow. After that, the class diagram once I really understand the data. So, moving forward I would most likely lean much more on going back and forth among the requirements document and the diagrams. So, nothing can be missed for sure.
